Three skills learned with reinforcement learning (movement, kick, get-up) and an orchestrator that runs seven robots, with a learned tactics layer on top. Everything below was measured in the official 2026 league server with our own C++ player.

Starting from nothing

In 2026 the RoboCup 3D simulation league moves to a new MuJoCo server with the Booster T1 humanoid: 23 joints, 31.6 kg, 7 a side, two halves of 5 minutes. No team brings a walk, a kick or a get-up from the old league. We started with a robot that could not stand and built every behaviour ourselves.

The plan: a library of learned skills, each trained and tested on its own, run by an orchestrator that decides who does what. One static C++ program per robot runs all of it, about 0.2 ms per decision, as the competition requires.

How every skill was trained

PPO with a recurrent policy8,192 robots per GPU8 H100s on the HPC, 2 RTX 6000 Ada on our workstation
1 · GPU physicsMuJoCo on the GPU (MJX with the Warp backend): thousands of T1 robots at once, with the server's own robot model, contacts and a one-cycle action delay.
2 · PPO updateA recurrent policy (GRU memory) improves on a reward per second of play. Penalties and randomisation phase in as it succeeds.
3 · Export to C++The network runs in our own plain C++ inside the player, matching training to within 0.003° per joint.
4 · League testsFixed batteries of tests in the real league server. A network replaces the final only if it passes everything its predecessor passed.

Movement network

A to B 7 of 7falls 0 of 30shoves 9 of 9

One network walks, turns, runs, stops and takes shoves. In a match the player gives it a target point (A to B) and it brakes to stop on the point. The omnidirectional tests give it a velocity command instead.

How it was trained

Constraints

Sees
83 numbers: joint angles and speeds, its last action, gyro, gravity direction, the velocity command, a gait clock, its own velocity.
Drives
21 joints (the head is separate): target = standing pose + 0.5 × output, PD kp 25, kd 0.6, 50 times a second, 20 to 40 ms action delay as in the server.
Practises
A-to-B legs of 4 to 35 m (45 % of episodes), flat-out sprints (30 %), fast runs (10 %), any direction (15 %). Half start from standing; half the runs end in a brake to a stop.
Ends
When the torso drops below 80 % of its standing height, or after 20 s.
Randomised
Shoves, start poses, landings after a beam, foot friction ±20 %, mass ±5 %, phased in as it succeeds.
Run
8,192 robots in parallel, 924 million steps, 84 minutes on one RTX 6000 Ada, warm-started through six runs from our first sprint network.

The movement network holds the robot still until the ball is placed. Then the kick network takes over and kicks in the commanded field direction. Clips end just before the server moves the ball back after a goal.

How it was trained

Constraints

Sees
The movement network's 83 numbers, plus the ball where its own head camera last saw it (25 times a second, ±60° view), time since it saw it, and the kick direction.
Drives
21 joints exactly as movement does, head held looking down, 50 times a second.
Starts
Ball still, 0.3 to 1.5 m away within ±45°; 30 % of episodes walking in. Aim within ±45° of the starting heading, always full power.
Ends
On a fall, after 8 s, or 2 s after the ball stops.
Randomised
Shoves, start poses, foot friction ±20 %, mass ±5 %. The ball is the server's own: 0.41 kg, 0.11 m radius.
Run
8,192 robots on one H100, 1.18 billion steps in 73 minutes, warm-started from movement through seven runs.

The ball terms only count once the ball rolls free, 0.4 m from the foot, so it is paid for the shot and not for pushing. The heavy lateral penalty and the 2° window took 27 m shots from 47 % to 84 %.

Shots at goal from 10 m

Ball 10 m from the goal centre, from 30° left to 30° right, dead ahead of the robot or 0.1 m to a side. A goal is the ball crossing the line between the posts.

Shots at goal from 27 m

The same test from 27 m, where the goal is 3.9° wide either side of the aim. Misses first. A miss that left nearly straight curved wide on the way.

Placed kicks

Ball 0.4, 0.8 or 1.2 m ahead, centred or 0.25 m to a side, kicked straight or 30° either way. Scored by the direction error against the commanded direction.

Get-up network

up within 5 s 26 of 26median 1.81 sfell again 0

Put on its back, front, sides or mid-fall, shoved over while standing or running, or dropped against a goalpost, the get-up network stands the robot up and hands back to the movement network. Done criterion: standing within 5 s in at least 95 % of tests, then 3 m of walking without a fall.

How it was trained

Constraints

Sees
76 numbers: joint angles and speeds, its last action, gyro, gravity direction, torso height.
Drives
All 23 joints: target = current angle + 0.6 × output, stiff gains (knee 60 N·m per rad, hip 45), 50 times a second.
Starts
From 41,088 fallen states made in the server's physics: on the back 28 %, front 28 %, each side 7 %, mid-fall after a shove 30 %.
Success
Both feet down, torso at standing height, tilt under 10°, still, held for 0.5 s. The episode ends there, or after 8 s.
Training aid
A lift on the torso of up to 60 % of body weight at first, faded to zero as it improved. Off in every test.
Run
8,192 robots, 816 million steps, 75 minutes on one RTX 6000 Ada; 3 h 20 min from the first get-up launch.

Not used: a fall penalty, hand penalties, scripted poses.

Every second on the ground costs, so it learns to get up fast with no hand-made motion.

The orchestrator: learned tactics over a rules team

policy z8b 243 in the league, w3h3 best in the simulatortrained about 25 min per run, 30 runs on 8 H100 and 16 A100simulator 1.0 M decisions/s per H100

Two layers decide what each of the seven robots does. The rules layer picks roles every cycle (keeper, the robot that plays the ball, formation spots for the rest), keeps the referee's set-play rules and drives the three skill networks. On top of it, a learned layer trained with reinforcement learning shifts each robot's spot and the ball player's aim. It was trained by self-play in our own GPU match simulator, set up with the skills' measured speed, kick spread and get-up time, starting from exactly the rules team. The rules layer wins every match below. The learned layer beats it in the simulator, and in the league server it is level with it so far.

Learned tactics
One small network shared by the six field robots, run on each robot's own view every 0.2 s: 52 inputs (own pose, ball, the teammates heard over team talk, the three nearest opponents seen, the play mode, time left), 3 outputs. Moves the robot's formation spot by up to 8 m and turns the ball player's aim by up to 60°. Output zero is the rules team exactly.

Rules orchestrator
Roles every cycle: player 1 keeps goal, the robot that can stand behind the ball first plays it (each robot says its time-to-ball in a 10-byte team-talk slot of its own), the rest hold formation spots that follow the ball. Plays either side, beams before kick-off and after goals, and keeps the referee's set-play rules: wide kick-offs since a direct kick-off goal does not count, 6.5 m away at their restarts, at most 2 robots in our goal area.

How it was trained

PPO with one network shared by the six field robots. The opponent is the rules team, or a frozen copy of the policy (self-play). The reward is goals for minus goals against, plus a small reward for moving the ball forward. Exploration noise jitters every robot's target 5 times a second, so play gets worse for the first iterations. The policy then learns to play through the noise, and its noise-free version climbs above the rules team. Each point on the chart is 256 simulated halves of 300 s against the rules team with the noise off.

  • w3h3: 8,192 games per GPU, exploration 0.3
  • w3h2: 4,096 games per GPU, exploration 0.3
  • w1h4: exploration 0.1, learning rate 1e-4

Goal difference per 300 s half against the rules team in the simulator, noise off, ± about 0.14. w3h3 (8,192 parallel games per GPU, exploration 0.3) reached +2.6. Runs with a third of that exploration (w1h4) settle near +0.6. The deployed z8b policy (5 min on the Z8, iteration 243) measured +1.31 ± 0.07 over 1,024 fresh halves: 62 % won, 18 % drawn, 19 % lost.

Highlight reels: every goal of every match

Each reel runs the whole match: open play fast-forwarded 8 times (marked x8), then real speed from 8 s before each goal to 3 s after it. Sides swap at half time, and the server colours the left team blue and the right team red, so our colour changes with the half.

The catch

  • FC Portugal and magmaOffenburg are their Portuguese Open 2026 binaries (archive.robocup.info), run on our machine. They are not necessarily the teams they will bring to RoboCup 2026.
  • FC Portugal's binary lost two of its seven players in each half (its own players exited mid-match), so 9-0 and 10-0 were against five to seven of their robots. magmaOffenburg kept all seven: 11-0 is the cleanest result.
  • All matches but the Apollo one ran in the server's synchronous mode, where it waits for every player each step. The Apollo match ran in real time, as in competition.
  • One match per opponent so far. Our robots still fall 32 to 48 times a match, mostly on contact and after the referee teleports a robot that stood too near the ball at the other side's restart.
  • The learned layer's gain in the simulator has not yet shown up in the league server.

Not solved yet

RL gain not shown in the league

+1.3 to +2.6 goals per half in our simulator. In the league against the rules layer: 41-47 over 13 matches, while two identical rules teams went 15-22 over 5. No measurable difference yet; next is adding the server's measured noise to the simulator and deciding on dozens of matches.

The gait shuffles

About 10 foot lifts a second, even standing (magmaOffenburg: 2.5 to 3). The rulebook bans unnatural behaviour that exploits the simulation. A still stance when idle is in testing; the real fix is retraining the gait with foot-timing terms.

Falls

32 to 48 per match across our 7 robots: contact, and referee teleports when a robot stands too close at the other side's restart.
